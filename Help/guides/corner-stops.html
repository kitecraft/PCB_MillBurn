Corner stops

Cut a locating corner into a sacrificial plate once. After that, every job on that size of stock starts the same way: push the stock into the corner, load the file, run. No edge finding, no touching off X and Y, no measuring. It is the most repeatable way to set up this machine, and it is the one to use when you are making the same board more than once.

This is a workshop job, not an app feature. Nothing here is generated for you — you cut the fixture yourself, once, with whatever CAM you already use, and your controller remembers where it is. That is the whole point: the fixture lives in the machine's own coordinates, so no software needs to own it, and it keeps working if you never open this program again.

What it is

A shallow tray milled into a plate that stays bolted to the table. The tray floor is where the stock lies. Three small pads, left standing at the plate's original height, poke up from the floor near two of the tray's walls — two on the front, one on the left. Their inner faces are the only three places the stock ever touches, and because the tray was cut by the machine itself, the machine already knows exactly where those faces are.

Why three pads and not two rails

The obvious build is a continuous L — two strips of aluminium screwed down at right angles. It works, and it is over-constrained. A 60 mm reference face has sixty millimetres of places for a chip or a burr to sit, and a single one of them tips the whole sheet. Worse, nothing tells you it happened: the stock still feels seated, still looks seated, and is a tenth or two out of square.

The fixturing answer to this is a hundred years old and is called 3-2-1 locating. Constrain each degree of freedom exactly once, and no more than once.

Degrees of freedomConstrained byHere
Z, and tip and tilt 3 points on the primary plane The tray floor, since the stock lies flat on it
Y, and rotation 2 stops on the secondary datum Two pads on the front, as far apart as the stock allows
X 1 stop on the tertiary datum One pad on the left, near the middle of the edge

Three pads is exactly constrained, which buys two things. Swarf between them falls into open space instead of under a reference face. And when something does get trapped, the stock lifts off one of the other pads, so the fault shows up as a gap you can see.

Sensible numbers to start from, all of which are yours to change:

DimensionStarting pointWhy that
Pad length, along the edge10–15 mmLong enough to be a face rather than a point; short enough that a chip usually misses it
Pad thickness, into the plate6–8 mmStiff enough not to deflect under a push, in MDF as well as aluminium
Spacing of the two front pads~80% of the edgeThe wider they are, the less a given error turns into rotation
Pad height above the tray floor1.0 mmFor 1.6 mm stock — see below, and check it
Position of the single X padcentred on its edgeNothing to gain from putting it anywhere else; one point is one point

If you would rather screw down a strip of aluminium than mill a plate, do — it is a real option and plenty of people work that way. Just know that you are choosing the less repeatable of the two, and that you cannot tell from looking when it has gone wrong.

Relieve the inside corner, or none of this works

This is the detail that decides whether the fixture is any good, and it is the one that is easiest to leave out.

A 3 mm end mill cannot cut a sharp inside corner. It leaves a 1.5 mm radius. Push a square stock corner into a radiused corner and it does not reach the two faces at all — it rides up on the fillet and sits several tenths out of position, silently, and by a different amount every time depending on how square that particular stock corner happens to be.

The fix is to cut the corner away entirely: a relief larger than the cutter radius, so both reference faces stay flat right up to the relief and the stock's own corner sits in fresh air and touches nothing. With three separate pads you get this for free, which is the third reason to prefer them.

How tall the pads are, and why it matters so much

The pads have to be lower than the top face of the stock. The outline pass runs the cutter along the stock edge, right beside them, and a stop standing 3 mm proud of a 1.6 mm board is a broken cutter and a ruined plate.

For 1.6 mm stock, cut the tray 1.0 mm deep. That is enough to stop the sheet, with 0.6 mm of the pad still safely below the work.

Work it out from your own stock rather than copying the number. Take your stock thickness and leave at least 0.5 mm of it above the pads:

StockTray depthLeft above the pads
1.6 mm1.0 mm0.6 mm
1.0 mm0.5 mm0.5 mm
0.8 mm0.3 mm0.5 mm

A fixture is cut for one stock thickness. Nothing checks this for you, and running 0.8 mm stock in a tray cut for 1.6 mm puts the pads 0.2 mm above the work. That is the number on this page that breaks a tool when it is wrong — so put the thickness on the plate, in the label below, and read it before you clamp anything.

Cutting the plate — once, ever

About four minutes of cutting. Do it carefully, because everything afterwards depends on it and nothing afterwards checks it.

  1. Bolt a sacrificial plate down — MDF, HDPE and aluminium all work; the plate only has to be flat, stable and not move again. It can be the spoilboard itself if the spoilboard is bolted rather than taped.
  2. Surface it flat in the machine, with a facing pass over at least the area the tray will occupy. This matters twice over: the tray floor and the pad tops both come from this surface, so if it is not flat, the pad heights are not what you asked for and the stock does not lie flat either.
  3. Home the machine. Everything below ends up stored as a machine coordinate, and that is only as good as your ability to return to the same frame next week. If your machine has homing switches, use them — this is exactly what they are for. If it has none, read when not to bother at the end of this page first.
  4. Draw the tray in whatever CAM you already use. Fusion, FreeCAD, Estlcam, Carbide Create — any of them will pocket a shape. Make it your stock size plus a millimetre or two of slack on the back and right, and only on the back and right: the front and left are the datum and must stay exactly where you put them. Leave the three pads standing as islands inside that pocket. There is no shape here that needs anything clever.
  5. Look at the file before you cut it. File › Open G-code in this app reads any .nc, whoever wrote it, and draws what it will do — or use your sender's own preview. You are about to cut into the one plate every future job is referenced to.
  6. Fit a flat end mill and cut it. 3 mm or 6 mm, either is fine; just size the corner relief for the one you actually fit, as above.
  7. Deburr and blow it out. Run a knife along the top edge of each pad and the edge of the tray floor. A raised burr on a pad face is worth a tenth of a millimetre and is invisible.

No CAM that does islands? Use rectangles. Pocket the plain stock-sized rectangle 1.0 mm deep, which leaves you a continuous L of wall on the front and left. Then take that wall away everywhere except at the three pads, with three or four more shallow rectangular pockets along the same two edges. Every cut is a rectangle, the result is identical, and the corner relief falls out of it because the wall is gone at the corner.

Record the datum, and write it on the plate

This is the half that makes the fixture worth having, and it lives in your controller rather than in any software. Seat a piece of stock in the corner, find its lower-left corner in X and Y the way you normally would — edge finder, a pin, or touching off on the two edges — and save that position as a work offset: G54, G55, whatever your sender offers. That offset is the fixture.

It is the same corner this app calls work zero, so nothing needs translating: select the offset and the program's origin is already where the stock's corner will be.

Then write the plate's own numbers on its face — engraved if you feel like cutting them, a permanent marker if you do not:

That is not decoration. Six months from now, on a Sunday, with the spoilboard resurfaced twice and three plates in a drawer, it is the only thing that answers which jig is this, what thickness is it cut for, and where is its zero. Write the machine coordinates down as well as the offset number: offsets get overwritten by accident, and the numbers let you put it back.

Using it — every job after that

This is the part that pays for the four minutes.

  1. Drop the stock in the tray and push it into the corner. One firm push along the diagonal, towards the corner, with the same pressure every time. Not a shove — you are seating a sheet against three pads, not clamping it.
  2. Clamp or tape it down without disturbing it, and check it is still against all three pads afterwards. Tightening a clamp is the usual way stock walks off a datum.
  3. Select the fixture's work offset — the G55, or whichever you saved — and set Z on the stock top. That is all. X and Y come from the fixture, so there is no edge finding and no touching off in the plane, on this job or any later one.
  4. Run isolate, drill, cut out. When a tool change forces you to set Z again, touch off on the same spot every time.

Check the outline clears the pads — nothing else will. The fixture references the stock, not the finished board, and the outline pass cuts inside the stock, so in a correctly laid-out job the cutter never comes near a pad. But a board placed hard against the edge of its stock will run the outline straight through one, and neither this app nor your sender knows the fixture exists. Leave a few millimetres of margin on the two datum edges, and press F5 to look at the toolpath against the stock before you cut.

A board that has already been cut out is a different problem, and a corner stop is not the answer to it.

How accurate is it, honestly

Very repeatable, and less accurate than you would guess — and the two are not the same thing. The fixture itself contributes almost nothing, because it was not measured, it was defined: the machine cut it in its own coordinates. Nearly all the error is the stock.

SourceContribution
Fixture position~0 — defined, not measured
Sheared or sawn FR4 edge, out of straight0.1–0.25 mm — dominant
Milled datum edges (see below)0.02–0.05 mm
Burrs, swarf, inconsistent push0.05 mm

So a corner stop against stock as supplied lands around ±0.15–0.25 mm. That is excellent for drilling and for cutting out, and it repeats session after session, which is why this is the recommended way to set up a repeat job. It is not the 50 µm that a pinned double-sided board reaches, and you should not lay out a 0.2 mm trace expecting it.

Square the stock and the whole budget collapses. The error is a crooked stock edge, so mill the two datum edges on the machine as the job's first cut. They are then true by construction, and ±0.15–0.25 mm becomes ±0.05 mm. It costs one pass and a few millimetres of stock, and it is the single highest-value thing you can add to this workflow.

Double-sided work needs a second corner

Flipping the stock swaps which physical edge meets which pad, so one corner cannot register both sides. The answer is a second corner stop, mirrored, on the same plate. Draw both while you are in the CAM anyway and cut them in one setup: it costs a few more minutes of machine time and nothing else, and doing it later means re-establishing the first one's datum to place the second against.

Save it as its own work offset — G55 and G56, say — and write both on the plate. The flip then becomes: turn the stock over, seat it in the other corner, switch offset, re-zero Z.

A four-sided nest pocket would also survive the flip, but only with a clearance fit — and clearance is slop. Two exactly-constrained corners beat one loose box.

This is not a replacement for pinning. If you need the two sides of a double-sided board to line up to tens of microns, drill before you flip and pin through the holes you already made. A mirrored corner stop is the convenient answer, not the accurate one; use it when 0.15 mm between the sides is fine, which for through-hole work it usually is.

When not to bother

  • A one-off board. The fixture takes four minutes plus a facing pass, and setting work zero by eye takes one. Cut a corner stop when you expect to come back to that stock size.
  • Stock smaller than the board. Nothing to push against on two edges.
  • A machine without repeatable homing. The datum is a machine coordinate held in a work offset. If you cannot return to the same machine frame next session, last session's offset points somewhere else, and you are better off with something you re-find each time.
  • Stock of a thickness the tray was not cut for. The pads end up too low to stop the sheet, or — worse — standing above it. One fixture, one thickness.
  • Stock you cannot get flat on the tray floor. Bowed single-sided stock lifts in the middle; the corner will locate it perfectly and the cut depth will still be wrong. That is a height map problem, not a fixturing one.
